An ill-timed message sent on a serviceless tract of road between Grenoble and Gstaad hovered in limbo. The blue progress bar imperceptibly crawled as airplane mode was toggled on and off, wondering if the delay had something to do with improperly configured international roaming or if it was just a simple misalignment between satellite and phone.

[a]

Tossing the device in the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter van’s armrest cupholder, the countryside rolled by. Through heavily tinted glass, the landscape looked dull. The primary impression the window provided was forward motion. The uniform, high-speed repetition of trees rendered the view as an unending loop.

A hotel replaced the trees. Recessed lights pinpricked the porte cochère at precise intervals but the impression was regardless diffuse and dull through the window, each a singular node with faint edges pushing outward. Crouching to exit the car door, he was shepherded into the luxuriously airy, conspicuously non-fragrant lobby. Although modern, this hotel had also borrowed from a more traditional “classy” aesthetic, referencing an Edwardian time gone by: well-suited bellhops attended white-oak-paneled elevators with steel buttons. The chandelier hanging from the triple-height ceiling was not draped in crystals, but instead a mass of geometric LED ribbons overlapping and knotting through each other, casting weird shadows on the glass and concrete table below.

[b]

It appeared there had been an issue with the booking. His assistant schizophrenically oscillated between effusive apology when facing him, and a repulsive demanding coldness with the employee. Instead of watching the unbecoming feedback loop with the French national, he sat at the bar waiting. His face was not famous, but the attendant was able to surmise he was a well-resourced man who exerted a great deal of influence in the universe of this weekend’s conference. So, relatively quickly, an agreement was reached. Apparently a bellhop had already brought his bags to the room, where the drink was finished.

While conspicuously, chicly, and understatedly luxurious, the room was not large. A bed faced a desk jammed in the corner. No comfortable posture escaped a view of the workstation, so he chose to sit there briefly, reviewing tomorrow's deck.

He was the CTO of Fever, a live-entertainment company, best known for Van Gogh: The Immersive Experience. This weekend, a conference in Grenoble presented an opportunity to raise funds for a new, unnamed venture that took Fever’s immersive technical setup and applied it to user’s personal data. Instead of seeing technicolor sunflowers dance across the room, images from visitors' phones and social media were analyzed and arranged to create a narrative spanning their life, played out across huge, hangar-like industrial spaces.

Flipping through the deck assembled by a junior analyst at a consulting firm, he made notes on the verbiage and diagrams. For example, he added a comment to a bubble titled “ANALYZE PHOTOGRAPH SENTIMENT” that read “split this into 3 steps: 1.) analyze photo content which then branches into 2.) reference user profile vector 3.) determine personalized sentiment.”

He got up, looked out the window, sat back down on the bed and opened a dating app. In the app, his location was set to San Francisco, despite primarily being based in New York. He felt that he held a stronger position in the west coast sexual food web, which was predicated less on cultural cache than resources. This specific dating app marketed itself as “connecting open-minded people exploring love, desire, and alternative relationships in a safe, inclusive space.” It differed from Tinder or Hinge in that many people used biographies that included phrases such as “Hierarchical Ethically Non Monogamous” or “Kinky switch 🖤” or “in therapy (and you should be too)” to self-describe. He zoomed in on a girl at a music festival dressed in a feather and nylon two piece set accessorized with what looked like mad scientist/aviator goggles. He wondered what it would be like to go to an event like that and sent her a “tap.”

Again, he looked out the window for a few seconds before checking his phone, which displayed no notifications. Pressing and holding the camera icon, a small haptic response buzzed in his hand as the app opened. He needed to shave, he realized, heading to the front desk to acquire a razor. Interfacing perfunctorily with the attendant (not explicitly rudely, just efficiently), he noticed his assistant and the front desk clerk from earlier sharing a drink. The two men were clearly enthralled, focused intensely on each other in a way that appeared to him uncomfortably intimate.

He watched for around 10 seconds, and took a discreet photo for no reason. Later, back upstairs, he shaved and fell asleep.

Slack-jawed and stimulated, the user’s physical environment is primed to recede in deference to the endlessly engaging immaterial digital, which begs aggressively for attention, morphing itself indiscriminately towards that end. Through an infinite, structured mutability, the digital is able to  transform attention into post-post-fiat capital. As Byung-Chul Han summarizes in Saving Beauty, “[the digital] only permits consumable, usable differences. Alterity gives way to diversity.”

At scale, Big Tech has taken the past decade and a half perfecting how to maximize the digital’s “consumable, usable differences.” In a hamfisted approach, platforms pummel sensational responses out of their users, bashing emotional receptors to oblivion in the hope of extracting more time.

As Franco Berardi writes in And: Phenomenology of the End: “The infinite complexity of phenomena overwhelms our capacity for observation.” What is overwhelming and unbearable is more easily read through the diagram. There is safety in a bounded understanding of an ineffable reality: think Gnostic Ophite diagrams or the Jain Aḍhāī-dvīpa. Today, insofar as digital platforms are born-diagrammatic—operating off of rule-based information flows—there is not only a safety, but an apparent accuracy in applying a structuralist mode of analysis to modern life.

In response, recent potent and celebrated critiques of digital culture draw on structuralism as their mode of analysis. Thinkers in this vein look at cybernetic vectors of information and read the world as a causal substrate. Especially in the “age of AI,” meaning is understood at the service of pseudo-mechanical systems.

Yet, in “Structuralism (Neo- or Paleo-),” Alexander Galloway writes: “In crass terms: if structuralism was correct, then LLMs are already AGI, case closed full stop. If structuralism was correct, then LLMs would generate meaning, they would harbor an unconscious, they would participate in the symbolic order. Yet, since LLMs don't do any of those things, structuralism has a lot of explaining to do.”

While the structures of cyberspace, political power, or capital are able to be understood (quite well) using these axioms, there still exists an organic, metaphysical, transcendental nucleus of perception that cannot be captured (although possibly well-modeled) by a purely structural point of view. In fact, a meaningful, politically useful, aesthetically powerful, and rarely deployed engagement with modern technologies utilizes a nuanced attenuation to the more squishy capacities of thought, the transcendental and the sublime. Although machines may occupy a Turing-complete cosmology, humans, their subjects, do not.

Heidegger’s The End of Philosophy and the Task of Thinking argues that although cybernetics has ended the classical metaphysical project in favor of efficiency and results, he affirms, “doesn't the insistence on what is demonstrable block the way to what-is? Perhaps there is a thinking which is more sober than the irresistible race of rationalization and the sweeping character of cybernetics. Presumably it is precisely this sweeping quality which is extremely irrational.”

There is regardless an insidious cultural desire bubbling, marketing structuralism as the modus operandi not just for certain types of analysis, but existence writ large. Heidegger's “what-is” is perverted as calculable. For example, Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, famously tweeted “i am a stochastic parrot, and so r u,” drawing a comparison between the algorithmic functioning of LLMs and human thought.  

Insofar as algorithms are clearly not transcendental apparatuses—in fact, they are so banal and prosaic that interacting with them often relegates one to the role of middle manager—those with stakes in elevating the machine’s import can find an easy argument in structurally debasing personhood. For Big Tech, Heidegger’s “what-is” is a highly profitable structure. A divergent future is a threat to that carefully built reality.

Thankfully, because structuralism will never fully encapsulate reality, there exist inevitable cracks through which media and affect can be freed from being wholly operational.

Few of us will ever enter a data center or see an undersea internet cable. We will, however, sit in a nondescript chair, near a fake potted plant, at an Ikea desk—or some similar permutation—and feel their infrastructural effects.

The term “AirSpace” was popularized in the mid-2010s to describe the increasingly-common flat, could-be-anywhere, aesthetics of Airbnbs, WeWorks, podcasting studios, coffee shops, etc. The concept adapted Rem Koolhaas’ Junkspace, with its modifier, Kyle Chayka, locating the divergence in an algorithmic flattening of culture: “The connective emotional grid of social media platforms is what drives the impression of AirSpace. If taste is globalized, then the logical endpoint is a world in which aesthetic diversity decreases.” Reminiscent of today’s structuralist drive, Koolhaas’ original essay identified a system so sprawling and byzantine that “only the diagram gives a bearable version.” Whereas Junkspace was only bearable via the diagram, AirSpace is not only bearable via diagrammatics, but also born from it.[1] It is the completion of Junkspace’s project, merging the work-appropriate video conference backdrop and the boudoir. Per Koolhaas, “we will be able to do anything anywhere. We will have conquered place. At the end of Junkspace, the Universal?”

 

Try as some might to structuralize ad infinitum, however, even within the most structural—the digital networks—AirSpace is proof that experience is still mediated through and oriented towards the fleshy sensory perception of the body. The gap in that disjoint moment of cybernetically digital and phenomenologically grounded may, then, provide insight into the failure of a purely structural mode of existence.

Although AirSpace plays at some universalizing ideal, consolidating taste to its most efficient and reproducible, there are phenomenological peculiarities that defy it at every turn. As Theodor Adorno puts it in Aesthetic Theory, natural (a term not solely referencing the colloquially understood “natural world”) beauty is “the trace of the non-identical in things under the spell of universal identity.” To find the beauty in AirSpace, pay attention to the peculiarity of the chair beneath your ass. Remember that each statistic or machine learning vector can trace itself back to a human creation born in a space like this. For one, all of the images in this essay were taken by a human, and later dumped into a training set for AI models. The digital feeds off the blurry, affective, hyper-indexical reality of life. Each realignment of the algorithm comes from a moment: a split second extra of attention that a real person—maybe prone in bed with the blanket slipping off and their overhead light buzzing quietly— cybernetically contributed to the training data. AirSpace represents that even within the most digitally mediated physical spaces, the phenomenological relay zone between computational and fleshy is more complex than a diagram can encode. The flaking edges of a human-assembled, flat-packed furniture interrupts the doomscroll ever so slightly.

Although it may prompt sensations, the digital cannot—even at the height of its physical manifestation in AirSpace— completely capture the physical moment’s affective reality. A “transcendental unity of apperception” is far beyond binary and vectors.
